## KP22 Robust Regression and Ridge Regression

**One-sentence summary**: Least squares is terrified of outliers; with a loss that goes easy on large errors, one bad point no longer drags the line away. Huber turns large errors linear, the ε-insensitive loss ignores small errors (and gains sparsity), and ridge regression takes ε=0 and gains a closed form.

### Prerequisites and symbols

**A few things you need first** (one plain sentence each):

- **Regression and least squares**: predict a real number with a line (or curve); choose the line that minimizes the sum of the squares of "prediction minus target".
- **Residual and cost**: the residual $e=y-g(x)$ is the target minus the prediction; a loss function gives each residual a penalty, and the cost is the sum of all penalties.
- **SVM and kernels** (KP19): a classifier that relies on a few support vectors, and a kernel in place of the inner product.
- **Linear algebra**: matrix products and inverses.

**Symbol table** (fixed for this lecture; note that in regression $y_i$ is a real target, not the class $\pm1$ of KP19 and KP20):

| Symbol | Meaning | Note |
|---|---|---|
| $x_i,y_i$ | input and real target of the $i$-th point | $N$ points |
| $g(x)$ | prediction function | for the linear case $g(x)=\langle w,x\rangle+w_0$ |
| $e$ | residual | $e=y-g(x)$ |
| $c$ | the Huber threshold | square below it, linear above |
| $\epsilon$ | width of the ε-insensitive tube | an error of at most $\epsilon$ is not penalized |
| $\xi_i,\xi_i^\star$ | slack variables | how far a point is above, below the tube |
| $\lambda_i,\lambda_i^\star$ | weights | for a point above, below the tube; at most one of the two is nonzero |
| $C$ | violation cost | the penalty weight of the SVR and also the upper limit of $\lambda$ |
| $\mathcal K$ | Gram matrix | $\mathcal K_{ij}=K(x_i,x_j)$, $N\times N$ |
| $\mathcal C$ | regularization coefficient of ridge regression | $\mathcal C=1/(2C)$ |
| $\phi(x),K$ | map and kernel | as in KP19 |

### 1. Outliers

**Plain explanation**: What does least squares do when the data contain one badly wrong point?

- **Outlier**: a point far from the trend of the others (an extreme value or a mislabelled target); it matters more at the edge of the $x$ range.

**Experiment setup** (designed here, generated by a program): 20 points with $x$ uniform on $[0,4]$ (random seed 0), $y=2x+1+{}$noise, noise standard deviation $0.3$. Add one outlier $(x,y)=(5,21)$, that is, $y$ 10 above the trend value 11. The true slope is 2 and the intercept 1.

**Result**:

| Data | Least-squares slope | Least-squares intercept |
|---|---:|---:|
| clean 20 points | 2.009 | 1.000 |
| with the outlier, 21 points | 2.686 | $-0.007$ |

With three other noise draws (seeds 0, 1, 2) the slope with the outlier is 2.69, 2.95, 2.98, and on clean data 2.009, 2.000, 1.972.

**Why**: squaring makes the contribution of a large error grow quadratically (an error of 10 costs 100 times an error of 1), so the solution is dominated by this one point.

![The outlier](../../assets/figures/lec05-kp22-outlier.png)

### 2. Robust losses

**Robust loss**: a loss function that goes easy on large errors so that a few bad points no longer dominate the solution. Two common ones:

- **ε-insensitive loss**: $\max(0,\ |e|-\epsilon)$. A point whose error is at most $\epsilon$ costs nothing; the excess grows linearly.
- **Huber loss**:

$$
\mathcal L=\begin{cases}\tfrac12e^2 & |e|\le c\\ c|e|-\tfrac{c^2}{2} & |e|>c\end{cases}
$$

Small errors ($|e|\le c$) are squared, as in least squares; large errors are made linear, so the effect of an outlier drops from quadratic to linear. The two pieces meet at $|e|=c$: $\tfrac12c^2=c\cdot c-\tfrac{c^2}{2}$.

**Values** (Huber with $c=1$, ε-insensitive with $\epsilon=0.5$, the square taken as $\tfrac12e^2$):

| Residual $e$ | Square | Huber | ε-insensitive |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 4.5 | 2.5 | 2.5 |
| 10 | 50 | 9.5 | 9.5 |

Figure `lec05-kp22-losses`: look first at the three curves coinciding near small errors (except the ε-insensitive, which is 0 inside the tube), then at the square racing up fastest for large errors while Huber and ε-insensitive only rise linearly. [Function: 1, 2]

![Robust losses](../../assets/figures/lec05-kp22-losses.png)

The next question: with the Huber loss, is the fitted line really less likely to be dragged away?

### 3. The Huber experiment

**How to solve it**: the Huber loss has no simple formula solution, so use **IRLS (iteratively reweighted least squares)**:

1. Start with an ordinary least-squares line.
2. Compute the residual $e$ of every point and give weights: weight 1 if $|e|\le c$, otherwise $c/|e|$ (the larger the error, the smaller the weight).
3. Solve one weighted least-squares problem with these weights, $(A^TWA)w=A^TWy$.
4. Go back to step 2 and repeat 100 times (in this experiment; in practice one can stop on convergence).

**Result** (the same 21 points as section 1, $c=1$):

| Method | Slope | Intercept |
|---|---:|---:|
| Huber | 2.099 | 0.867 |
| Least squares (with the outlier) | 2.686 | $-0.007$ |
| Least squares (clean data) | 2.009 | 1.000 |

Three noise draws (seeds 0, 1, 2): Huber slopes 2.10, 2.14, 2.13; least squares 2.69, 2.95, 2.98.

**How to read it**: Huber is still a little off (the truth is 2), but much less: the weight of the outlier is pressed down to $c/|e|$.

Figure `lec05-kp22-hfit`: look at the same 21 points, then compare the two lines: the Huber line hugs the trend of the clean data and the least-squares line is pulled up by the outlier. [Function: 1, 2]

![The Huber experiment](../../assets/figures/lec05-kp22-hfit.png)

### 4. ε-insensitive regression: ignore small errors and gain sparsity

**Plain explanation**: draw a "tube" of half-width $\epsilon$ above and below the predicted line. Points inside the tube have an error within the tolerance and need no weight; only points on the edge of the tube or outside it have weights, and they are the support vectors.

**Formal definition of the SVR (support vector regression)**: minimize

$$
\tfrac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_i(\xi_i+\xi_i^\star),
$$

subject to the error of every point being at most the tube width plus slack: $|y_i-\langle w,\phi(x_i)\rangle-w_0|\le\epsilon+\text{slack}$. $\xi_i$ and $\xi_i^\star$ are how far a point lies above and below the tube. The first term keeps the line gentle, and the second penalizes the part outside the tube.

The prediction function that comes out is

$$
g(x)=\sum_i(\lambda_i^\star-\lambda_i)K(x,x_i)+w_0 .
$$

**Reading the result with the KKT conditions**. The KKT (Karush–Kuhn–Tucker) conditions are the conditions an optimal solution must satisfy; read here:

- Point error $<\epsilon$ (inside the tube): $\lambda_i=\lambda_i^\star=0$, not a support vector.
- Point error $\ge\epsilon$ (on the edge or outside): a support vector.
- Point error $>\epsilon$ (outside the tube): the weight hits the limit, $\lambda=C$.

So $\epsilon$ is the knob of sparsity: the wider the tube, the more points inside it and the fewer the support vectors.

Figure `lec05-kp22-svr`: look at the objective and constraints first, then at the sketch of the tube on the right: points inside have no weight and only points on the edge or outside do. [Function: 1, 3]

![ε-insensitive regression](../../assets/figures/lec05-kp22-svr.png)

### 5. The effect of ε

**Experiment setup** (designed here, generated by a program): $x$ uniform on $[0,2\pi]$, 60 points (random seed 1), $y=\sin x+{}$noise with standard deviation $0.15$. An SVR with the RBF kernel (scikit-learn), $\gamma=0.5$, $C=10$, sweeping $\epsilon$. The error is the mean squared error (MSE) between the prediction curve and the true $\sin$ on 300 equally spaced points.

| $\epsilon$ | Support vectors | MSE against the true sin | Share of training points inside the tube |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.01 | 58 | 0.0008 | 13% |
| 0.1 | 29 | 0.0020 | 63% |
| 0.2 | 9 | 0.0038 | 90% |
| 0.3 | 6 | 0.0175 | 97% |
| 0.5 | 5 | 0.0551 | 97% |
| 1.0 | 2 | 0.3455 | 100% |

**How to read it**: the larger $\epsilon$ is, the fewer support vectors (58 down to 2), but the cruder the solution: the MSE rises from 0.0008 to 0.3455. When the tube is wider than the amplitude of the sine, the curve is nearly flattened.

### 6. Ridge regression: ε=0 gives a closed form

**Plain explanation**: ridge regression is "least squares with a norm penalty": besides the squared prediction error it also penalizes the length of the solution so that the coefficients do not get too large.

**How to get it from the SVR**:

1. Set $\epsilon=0$ (no tube) and $w_0=0$.
2. Change the inequality constraints into equalities: the error of every point equals its slack $\xi_i$ exactly.
3. The objective becomes minimizing $\mathcal C\|w\|^2+\sum_i\xi_i^2$ with $\mathcal C=\tfrac1{2C}$.

**Closed form** (no iteration needed):

$$
g(x)=y^T(\mathcal K+\mathcal CI)^{-1}p ,
$$

where $y=(y_1,\dots,y_N)^T$ is the target vector and $p=(K(x,x_1),\dots,K(x,x_N))^T$. It differs from the kernel least squares of KP21 only by the term $\mathcal CI$.

**The price**: with $\epsilon=0$ there is no point whose small error "contributes nothing", every point has a weight and sparsity is lost (the loss changed from the absolute value to the square and the constraints into equalities); there is no free lunch.

### 7. Collinearity and stability

**Two forms for the linear kernel**: with the data matrix $X$ (one point per row),

$$
w=(X^TX+\mathcal CI)^{-1}X^Ty=X^T(XX^T+\mathcal CI)^{-1}y .
$$

The left form solves a system of the size of the number of features, the right one a system of the size of the number of points; with many features and few points the right one is cheaper.

**Check** (designed here): 15 points, 4 features (random seed 2, standard normal), $y$ also standard normal, $\mathcal C=0.8$: the $w$ computed by the two forms differ by at most $6\times10^{-16}$ (rounding error).

**A collinear example** (designed here): 30 points (seed 3), $t$ standard normal, two columns $(t,\ t+10^{-4}\cdot\text{noise})$ almost identical, $y=t+0.1\cdot\text{noise}$.

| $\mathcal C$ | Coefficients $w$ | Condition number of $X^TX+\mathcal CI$ |
|---:|---|---:|
| 0 (ordinary least squares) | $(-170.0,\ 171.0)$ | $5\times10^8$ |
| 0.1 | $(0.49,\ 0.49)$ | 776 |

The **condition number** is the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix; the larger it is, the more a small disturbance of the data makes the solution jump. When two columns are almost the same, least squares can only cancel two huge opposite coefficients against each other, which is unstable. Adding a small positive number $\mathcal C$ along the diagonal brings the condition number down to 776 and the coefficients back to a sensible $(0.49,0.49)$.

This is a common numerical stabilization; the price is that the coefficients are pressed towards 0.

### 8. Comparing the four methods

The same 21 points with the outlier (section 1), slope (truth 2):

| Method | Setting | Slope | Nonzero coefficients (support vectors) |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Least squares | — | 2.686 | 21 |
| Huber | $c=1$ | 2.099 | 21 |
| ε-insensitive (linear SVR) | $\epsilon=0.5$, $C=10$ | 2.122 | 4 |
| Ridge regression | $\mathcal C=0.1$ | 2.679 | 21 |

**To remember**: Huber = large errors made linear; ε-insensitive = small errors ignored (sparse); ridge = $\epsilon=0$ gives a closed form (sparsity lost).

**Common misconception**: ridge regression resists outliers. In fact it resists collinearity and overfitting, not outliers (slope 2.679, almost the same as the 2.686 of least squares).

### Extension: the threshold c, practice and worked solution

**The Huber threshold $c$** (the same 21 points):

| $c$ | 0.2 | 0.5 | 1 | 2 | 5 | 20 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Slope | 2.045 | 2.054 | 2.099 | 2.188 | 2.455 | 2.686 |

With a large $c$ every error lies in the squared zone and it equals least squares; with a small $c$ the outlier is nearly ignored. $c$ must be chosen to match the noise size (here the standard deviation 0.3).
