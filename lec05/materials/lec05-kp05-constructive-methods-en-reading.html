KP05 Constructive Methods: Growing a Network from Training Points

One-sentence summary: In practice we only have labelled points. A constructive method first splits them with a master unit and then handles the groups that are still mixed with ancillary units until different classes no longer share a code; this is a training-set goal, not a guarantee on unseen data.

1. In practice we only have labelled points: first split them with a master unit

Plain explanation: The construction of KP04 knew the equations of the target regions, while in practice we only have points and labels. This section takes the four XOR points and starts building from points.

  • Master unit: the first unit of a layer, in charge of classifying; it splits all points into a group with output 1 and a group with output 0.

Formal definition: let X={(xn,cn)}n=1NX=\{(\mathbf x_n,c_n)\}_{n=1}^NX={(xn​,cn​)}n=1N​ be a finite labelled set with cn∈{0,1}c_n\in\{0,1\}cn​∈{0,1}. Here z1=f(x1+x2−0.5)z_1=f(x_1+x_2-0.5)z1​=f(x1​+x2​−0.5), where f(t)=1f(t)=1f(t)=1 if t>0t>0t>0 and 0 otherwise.

2. The master gets only 3 of 4 right: its 1 group holds two A and one B

Mixed group: an output group that contains points of both classes. (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) alone is in the 0 group (B, correct). The 1 group is (0,1)(0,1)(0,1), (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) and (1,1)(1,1)(1,1), all called A: the two A are right and the B at (1,1)(1,1)(1,1) is wrong. The master gets 3 points right.

Figure lec05-kp05-mixed: red rings mark the three points of the mixed group.

3. For the demonstration we pick one ancillary line by hand to split off the upper-right B

Ancillary unit: a new unit designed only for the points of a mixed group; it gives every point one more bit. Real methods find its weights with a training algorithm; here we pick them by hand.

(0,0)(0,0)(0,0) is not in the mixed group and takes no part in training; its z2=f(1.5)=1z_2=f(1.5)=1z2​=f(1.5)=1 is just a value computed along the way. This is a mechanism demonstration, not a recorded run of a training algorithm.

4. With the extra bit the codes are 01, 11, 11, 10

Purification: adding bits so that points of different classes no longer share exactly the same code.

Input Code z1z2z_1z_2z1​z2​ Label
(0,0)(0,0)(0,0) 01 B
(0,1)(0,1)(0,1) 11 A
(1,0)(1,0)(1,0) 11 A
(1,1)(1,1)(1,1) 10 B

Purification does not require one code per class: B occupying the two codes 01 and 10 is fine.

6. Why it stops: the mixed group shrinks, correct points increase, and the number of points is finite

  • Progress one: within a layer, the mixed group that still needs refining strictly shrinks. Here it goes from 3 points to 0.
  • Progress two: under a suitable inter-layer construction the new master keeps the points the old one got right and gets at least one more. With CkC_kCk​ the number of points the master of layer kkk gets right:

Ck+1≥Ck+1,Ck≤N. C_{k+1}\ge C_k+1,\qquad C_k\le N. Ck+1​≥Ck​+1,Ck​≤N.

This condition is required by the cited construction, is not a general result verified by this example, and is not proved here; here CCC rises from 3 to 4. An integer cannot increase forever, so the process stops. Note that 3→0 counts points in the mixed group, not an error rate. This is the stopping reason when the conditions hold, not a claim that every nonseparable subgroup can be perfectly separated.

Figure lec05-kp05-progress: red is the number of points in the mixed group, blue is the number of correct points, the dashed line is N=4N=4N=4; the values are computed for this example, not an empirical curve from the source.

7. Stopping has conditions: labels must not contradict, and all correct only means these points

When two identical inputs carry different labels, a deterministic network has one output for one input and cannot get both right. Nodes must be added by rule to get strict progress; adding neurons arbitrarily does not guarantee termination.

Common misconception: since all training points end up right, new data will be right as well. All correct concerns only the points given; it guarantees neither correct answers at unseen locations nor a small network.

Extension: nearest neighbour, three layers, practice and worked solution

Another route (nearest neighbour): compare distances with the perpendicular bisector of two training points. [Added] Let u,v\mathbf u,\mathbf vu,v be two training points. The equal-distance condition expands, and the common quadratic term cancels:

∥x−u∥2=∥x−v∥2  ⟺  2(v−u)Tx=∥v∥2−∥u∥2. \|\mathbf x-\mathbf u\|^2=\|\mathbf x-\mathbf v\|^2\iff 2(\mathbf v-\mathbf u)^T\mathbf x=\|\mathbf v\|^2-\|\mathbf u\|^2. ∥x−u∥2=∥x−v∥2⟺2(v−u)Tx=∥v∥2−∥u∥2.

With u=(0,0)\mathbf u=(0,0)u=(0,0) and v=(1,0)\mathbf v=(1,0)v=(1,0) this gives 2x1=12x_1=12x1​=1, that is x1=0.5x_1=0.5x1​=0.5. This is only one pairwise comparison, not a full nearest-neighbour network experiment. NNN points have at most N(N−1)/2N(N-1)/2N(N−1)/2 pairs, so comparisons can grow quadratically; this does not mean all must be kept and it is not a minimal network size.

Figure lec05-kp05-nearest: two training points uuu, vvv and their perpendicular bisector x1=0.5x_1=0.5x1​=0.5.

Three layers suffice, which is not a bound on the growth: inputs after the first layer are binary codes, and KP04's detectors plus OR can classify the purified codes, so a suitable three-layer representation is enough; but a layer-by-layer growth procedure is not guaranteed to stop within three layers. Existence and a depth guarantee for a procedure are not the same theorem.

Figure lec05-kp05-layers: inputs of later layers are binary codes and detectors plus OR are enough; the growth procedure has no stated bound on the number of layers.

Practice:

  1. Group the four points by their full code, list the labels of each group and count the points in mixed groups. Hint: first write down which points have each of the codes 01, 11 and 10.
  2. If one input appears with contradictory labels, can adding more nodes classify both correctly? Hint: deterministic outputs.

Worked solution of questions 1 and 2: (1) By master bit: {(0,0)}\{(0,0)\}{(0,0)} is B, pure; {(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)}\{(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}{(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)} is A, A, B, mixed, 3 points. (2) By full code: 01: B; 11: A, A; 10: B, all pure, 0 mixed points. (3) 3→0 counts points still mixed; an error rate counts wrong answers, and the master alone is wrong on 1 point. (4) The same input gives the same output, so a deterministic network gives one answer and cannot be right for both A and B.
